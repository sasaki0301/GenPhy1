Inertia and Rotational Kinetic Energy

Learning Objectives

  • Review inertia as resistance to changes in motion.
  • Explain rotational inertia as resistance to changes in rotation.
  • Compare linear and rotational quantities.
  • Calculate rotational kinetic energy.
  • Describe why mass distribution affects rotation.
  • Apply rotational ideas to real-life examples such as wheels, disks, doors, and spinning skaters.

1. From Linear Motion to Rotational Motion

In previous chapters, we studied objects moving in straight lines and curved paths. Now we extend motion to objects that rotate. A wheel, fan blade, door, bicycle tire, and spinning skater all involve rotational motion.

Key Idea: Linear motion describes movement from place to place, while rotational motion describes spinning around an axis.
Linear Motion Rotational Motion
Mass resists changes in linear motion Rotational inertia resists changes in rotation
Velocity measures how fast position changes Angular velocity measures how fast angle changes
Kinetic energy = ½mv² Rotational kinetic energy = ½Iω²

2. What is Inertia?

Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist changes in its motion. A heavy object is harder to start moving, stop, or change direction because it has more inertia.

Review: Greater mass means greater inertia.

Example:

It is easier to push an empty cart than a cart full of books because the full cart has greater mass and therefore greater inertia.

3. What is Rotational Inertia?

Rotational inertia is the resistance of an object to changes in its rotational motion. It depends not only on mass, but also on how far the mass is distributed from the axis of rotation.

Key Idea: Rotational inertia increases when more mass is farther from the axis of rotation.

Mass farther from the axis is harder to rotate.

Example:

A long rod is harder to rotate when held at one end than when held near its center because more of its mass is farther from the rotation axis.

4. Moment of Inertia

The moment of inertia is the mathematical measure of rotational inertia. It is represented by the symbol I.

I = mr²

This simple formula applies to a point mass rotating at a distance r from an axis.

  • I = moment of inertia, measured in kg·m²
  • m = mass, measured in kg
  • r = distance from axis, measured in m
Important: Because radius is squared, moving mass farther from the axis greatly increases rotational inertia.

Example 1: Moment of Inertia of a Point Mass

A 2 kg object rotates 3 m from an axis. Find its moment of inertia.

I = mr²
I = (2)(3²)
I = (2)(9)
I = 18 kg·m²

5. Mass Distribution Matters

Two objects can have the same mass but different rotational inertia depending on how the mass is arranged.

Object Mass Distribution Rotational Inertia
Solid disk Mass spread throughout disk Lower than a ring of same mass and radius
Ring or hoop Mass concentrated near outer edge Higher than a solid disk of same mass and radius
Spinning skater with arms out Mass farther from axis Greater rotational inertia
Spinning skater with arms in Mass closer to axis Lower rotational inertia

A ring has greater rotational inertia than a solid disk of the same mass and radius because more mass is farther from the axis.

6. Angular Velocity

Angular velocity describes how fast an object rotates. It is represented by the symbol ω.

ω = angular velocity

The common SI unit of angular velocity is rad/s, or radians per second.

Simple Meaning: Greater angular velocity means faster spinning.

7. Rotational Kinetic Energy

An object that spins has rotational kinetic energy. This is similar to linear kinetic energy, but instead of mass and velocity, it uses moment of inertia and angular velocity.

KErot = ½Iω²
  • KErot = rotational kinetic energy, measured in joules
  • I = moment of inertia, measured in kg·m²
  • ω = angular velocity, measured in rad/s

Example 2: Calculating Rotational Kinetic Energy

A rotating object has moment of inertia 4 kg·m² and angular velocity 3 rad/s. Find its rotational kinetic energy.

KErot = ½Iω²
KErot = ½(4)(3²)
KErot = 2(9)
KErot = 18 J

8. Rolling Motion

A rolling object has two types of kinetic energy:

  • Linear kinetic energy because its center of mass moves forward
  • Rotational kinetic energy because it spins
Total KE = ½mv² + ½Iω²
Key Idea: Rolling motion combines translation and rotation.

A rolling wheel moves forward while also rotating.

Example:

A rolling ball has energy from moving forward and energy from spinning. This is why rolling objects can behave differently from sliding objects.

9. Torque (Moment of Force)

Torque is the turning effect of a force. It causes objects to rotate around an axis or pivot point.

Key Idea: Force causes linear acceleration, while torque causes rotational motion.
τ = rF sinθ

Where:

  • τ = torque (N·m)
  • r = distance from axis (m)
  • F = applied force (N)
  • θ = angle between force and lever arm
Important: Maximum torque occurs when the force is perpendicular to the lever arm.

Applying force farther from the pivot produces greater torque.

Situation Torque Effect
Pushing near hinge Smaller torque
Pushing far from hinge Larger torque
Force perpendicular to lever arm Maximum torque
Force directed toward pivot Zero torque

Example 2: Calculating Torque

A 20 N force is applied perpendicular to a wrench 0.5 m from the pivot. Find the torque.

τ = rF sinθ
τ = (0.5)(20)(sin90°)
τ = (0.5)(20)(1)
τ = 10 N·m

Example 3: Zero Torque

A force is applied directly toward the pivot point. What is the torque?

Since θ = 0°:

τ = rF sin0°
τ = 0

The torque is: 0 N·m

Concept Check: Why is it easier to open a door using the handle rather than pushing near the hinge?

The handle is farther from the pivot, producing greater torque for the same applied force.

10. Real-Life Applications

Application Rotational Concept
Figure skater pulling arms inward Decreases rotational inertia and spins faster
Heavy flywheel Stores rotational kinetic energy
Bicycle wheel Has both rotational and translational kinetic energy
Door opening Easier to rotate when pushed far from the hinge
Gymnast tucking during a flip Reduces rotational inertia and increases spin rate

Concept Check

1. Why is a ring harder to rotate than a solid disk of the same mass and radius?

Because more of the ring’s mass is farther from the axis, giving it greater rotational inertia.

2. What happens to rotational inertia when mass moves farther from the axis?

Rotational inertia increases.

3. Why does a spinning skater rotate faster when pulling their arms inward?

Pulling the arms inward decreases rotational inertia, allowing the skater to spin faster.

Practice Drills

Drill 1: A 3 kg point mass rotates 2 m from an axis. Find its moment of inertia.

I = mr²
I = (3)(2²)
I = (3)(4)
I = 12 kg·m²

Drill 2: An object has I = 6 kg·m² and angular velocity 2 rad/s. Find rotational kinetic energy.

KErot = ½Iω²
KErot = ½(6)(2²)
KErot = 3(4)
KErot = 12 J

Drill 3: If angular velocity doubles, what happens to rotational kinetic energy?

Rotational kinetic energy becomes four times larger because KErot depends on ω².

Checkpoint Challenge

A 2 kg point mass rotates 4 m from an axis at 3 rad/s. Find:
1. Moment of inertia
2. Rotational kinetic energy

1. Moment of inertia:
I = mr² = (2)(4²) = (2)(16) = 32 kg·m²

2. Rotational kinetic energy:
KErot = ½Iω²
KErot = ½(32)(3²)
KErot = 16(9)
KErot = 144 J

Pisay-Style Question: Two wheels have the same mass and radius. Wheel A has most of its mass near the center, while Wheel B has most of its mass near the rim. Which wheel is harder to start rotating, and why?

Wheel B is harder to start rotating because more of its mass is farther from the axis, giving it greater rotational inertia.

Lesson Summary

  • Inertia is resistance to changes in motion.
  • Rotational inertia is resistance to changes in rotational motion.
  • Moment of inertia depends on mass and distance from the axis.
  • Mass farther from the axis greatly increases rotational inertia.
  • Rotational kinetic energy is calculated using KErot = ½Iω².
  • Rolling objects have both translational and rotational kinetic energy.
  • Skaters and gymnasts spin faster by reducing rotational inertia.
