Newton’s Law of Universal Gravitation

Learning Objectives

  • Understand Newton’s Law of Universal Gravitation.
  • Identify factors affecting gravitational force.
  • Explain the inverse square relationship of gravity.
  • Solve problems involving gravitational force.
  • Relate gravity to planetary and satellite motion.

1. What is Gravity?

Gravity is the attractive force between any two objects with mass. Every object in the universe attracts every other object through gravity.

Key Idea: Gravity is a universal force that acts between all masses.

Gravity acts between all objects with mass.

Although gravity exists between all objects, it becomes noticeable only when at least one object has very large mass, such as Earth, the Moon, or the Sun.

2. Newton’s Law of Universal Gravitation

Isaac Newton proposed that every mass attracts every other mass through a gravitational force.

F = G(m₁m₂ / r²)
  • F = gravitational force (N)
  • G = universal gravitational constant
  • m₁ and m₂ = masses of objects (kg)
  • r = distance between centers of masses (m)
Important: Gravity becomes stronger with larger masses and weaker with greater distance.

Larger masses produce stronger gravitational attraction.

3. The Inverse Square Law

Gravity follows an inverse square relationship. This means that if distance doubles, gravitational force becomes four times smaller.

F ∝ 1/r²
Distance Change Effect on Gravity
Distance doubles Force becomes 1/4
Distance triples Force becomes 1/9
Distance halves Force becomes 4 times larger
Small increases in distance greatly reduce gravitational force.

4. Effect of Mass on Gravity

Increasing either mass increases gravitational force.

If one mass doubles, gravity doubles. If both masses double, gravity becomes four times larger.

Greater masses create stronger gravitational attraction.

5. Solving Gravitational Force Problems

Example 1

Two objects have masses 4 kg and 6 kg and are separated by 2 m. Use G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg². Find the gravitational force.

F = G(m₁m₂/r²)

F = (6.67 × 10⁻¹¹)(4)(6)/(2²)

F = (6.67 × 10⁻¹¹)(24)/4

F = (6.67 × 10⁻¹¹)(6)

F = 4.00 × 10⁻¹⁰ N

6. Gravity and Orbital Motion

Gravity keeps planets orbiting the Sun and moons orbiting planets. Without gravity, planets would move in straight lines.

Gravity provides the centripetal force needed for circular and elliptical orbits.

Gravity keeps planets in orbit around the Sun.

7. Gravity and Weight

Weight is the gravitational force acting on an object.

W = mg
  • W = weight (N)
  • m = mass (kg)
  • g = gravitational field strength (9.8 m/s² on Earth)
Mass remains constant, but weight changes depending on gravity.

Example 3

Find the weight of a 60 kg person on Earth.

W = mg
W = (60)(9.8)
W = 588 N

8. Satellites and Free Fall

Satellites remain in orbit because they are continuously falling toward Earth while moving forward fast enough to miss the surface.

Astronauts appear weightless because they are in continuous free fall.

Satellites remain in orbit due to gravity and forward motion.

Checkpoint Challenge

Two identical masses are separated by distance r. If both masses double and the distance becomes twice as large, what happens to gravitational force?

Doubling both masses makes gravity 4 times larger. Doubling distance makes gravity 4 times smaller. The effects cancel, so gravitational force stays the same.

Pisay-Style Question: Astronauts in orbit appear weightless even though gravity still acts on them. Why?

Astronauts and the spacecraft are both continuously falling toward Earth together, creating the sensation of weightlessness.

Lesson Summary

  • Gravity is the attractive force between masses.
  • Newton’s Law of Universal Gravitation describes gravitational force.
  • Gravity increases with mass and decreases with distance.
  • Gravity follows an inverse square law.
  • Gravity keeps planets and satellites in orbit.
  • Weight is the gravitational force acting on an object.
  • Satellites remain in orbit due to gravity and forward motion.
