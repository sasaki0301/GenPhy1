Simple Harmonic Motion

Learning Objectives

  • Define oscillation and simple harmonic motion.
  • Explain restoring force and equilibrium position.
  • Identify amplitude, period, and frequency.
  • Describe energy changes in SHM.
  • Interpret basic displacement-time graphs for oscillating systems.
  • Apply formulas for period and frequency in simple problems.

1. What is Oscillation?

Oscillation is a repeated back-and-forth motion around a central position. Examples include a swinging pendulum, a vibrating guitar string, a mass attached to a spring, and a child on a swing.

Key Idea: Oscillatory motion repeats over time.

An oscillating object moves back and forth around an equilibrium position.

2. Equilibrium Position and Restoring Force

The equilibrium position is the central position where the object naturally rests. When the object is displaced from equilibrium, a restoring force pulls it back toward the center.

Key Idea: Simple harmonic motion occurs when the restoring force is directed toward equilibrium and is proportional to displacement.
F = -kx

This is Hooke’s Law for a spring:

  • F = restoring force
  • k = spring constant
  • x = displacement from equilibrium
  • The negative sign means the force points opposite the displacement.

If a spring is stretched to the right, the spring pulls left. If compressed to the left, the spring pushes right. The force always tries to restore the system to equilibrium.

3. What Makes Motion Simple Harmonic?

Simple harmonic motion, or SHM, is a special type of oscillation where the restoring force becomes stronger as the object moves farther from equilibrium.

Position Restoring Force Speed
At maximum displacement Greatest Zero momentarily
At equilibrium Zero Maximum
Opposite maximum displacement Greatest in opposite direction Zero momentarily
Important: The object is fastest at equilibrium and slowest at the endpoints.

4. Amplitude, Period, and Frequency

Quantity Meaning Unit
Amplitude Maximum displacement from equilibrium m
Period Time for one complete oscillation s
Frequency Number of oscillations per second Hz
f = 1/T
T = 1/f

Example 1: Frequency from Period

A pendulum completes one oscillation in 0.50 s. Find its frequency.

f = 1/T
f = 1/0.50
f = 2 Hz

5. Simple Pendulum

A simple pendulum consists of a mass called a bob attached to a string. For small angles, a pendulum approximately undergoes simple harmonic motion.

T = 2π√(L/g)
  • T = period
  • L = length of pendulum
  • g = gravitational field strength
Important: For small swings, pendulum period mainly depends on string length, not mass.

A pendulum swings back and forth around its equilibrium position.

Example 2: Pendulum Reasoning

If the length of a pendulum increases, what happens to its period?

The period increases because:

T = 2π√(L/g)

A longer pendulum takes more time to complete one oscillation.

6. Mass-Spring System

A mass attached to a spring can also undergo SHM. When stretched or compressed, the spring exerts a restoring force.

T = 2π√(m/k)
  • T = period
  • m = mass
  • k = spring constant
For a spring system:
  • Larger mass → longer period
  • Stiffer spring → shorter period

A mass on a spring oscillates because the spring provides a restoring force.

7. Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion

Energy continuously transforms between potential energy and kinetic energy during SHM.

Position Kinetic Energy Potential Energy
At endpoint Minimum Maximum
At equilibrium Maximum Minimum
Key Idea: At the endpoints, the object momentarily stops. At equilibrium, it moves fastest.

8. Displacement-Time Graph of SHM

The displacement-time graph of simple harmonic motion is a repeating wave-like curve. It shows how position changes over time.

SHM produces a repeating displacement-time graph.

One complete cycle represents one period.

Concept Check

1. What is the equilibrium position?

It is the central resting position where the net restoring force is zero.

2. Where is speed greatest in SHM?

Speed is greatest at the equilibrium position.

3. Where is potential energy greatest in SHM?

Potential energy is greatest at the endpoints, where displacement is maximum.

Practice Drills

Drill 1: An oscillator has a period of 0.25 s. Find its frequency.

f = 1/T
f = 1/0.25
f = 4 Hz

Drill 2: A wave-like oscillation has frequency 5 Hz. Find its period.

T = 1/f
T = 1/5
T = 0.20 s

Drill 3: If a spring becomes stiffer, what happens to the period of a mass-spring system?

The period decreases because T = 2π√(m/k). A larger k makes the period smaller.

Checkpoint Challenge

A pendulum has frequency 0.5 Hz.
1. What is its period?
2. Does this mean it oscillates quickly or slowly compared with a 2 Hz oscillator?

1. T = 1/f = 1/0.5 = 2 s

2. It oscillates more slowly than a 2 Hz oscillator because it completes fewer cycles per second.

Pisay-Style Question: A pendulum bob is released from the left endpoint of its swing. Describe how its speed and energy change as it passes through equilibrium.

At the endpoint, speed is zero and potential energy is maximum. As it moves toward equilibrium, potential energy converts into kinetic energy. At equilibrium, speed and kinetic energy are maximum.

Lesson Summary

  • Oscillation is repeated back-and-forth motion.
  • Simple harmonic motion occurs when restoring force is proportional to displacement and directed toward equilibrium.
  • Amplitude is maximum displacement from equilibrium.
  • Period is the time for one complete cycle.
  • Frequency is the number of cycles per second.
  • Frequency and period are related by f = 1/T.
  • In SHM, speed is greatest at equilibrium and zero at endpoints.
  • Energy transforms between kinetic and potential energy during SHM.
  • Pendulum period depends mainly on length for small angles.
  • Spring period increases with mass and decreases with spring stiffness.
