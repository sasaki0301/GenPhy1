Chapter 1 Summary: Physics and Measurement

Chapter Overview

This chapter introduced the foundation of General Physics 1: understanding what physics studies and how measurements are used to describe the physical world. Physics becomes meaningful when observations are supported by numbers, units, and clear reasoning.

Big Idea: Physics explains natural phenomena through measurable quantities, mathematical relationships, and logical interpretation.

1. What Physics Studies

Physics is the study of matter, energy, motion, forces, and interactions. It helps explain everyday events such as falling objects, moving vehicles, sound waves, heat transfer, and planetary motion.

Examples of Physics Questions:
  • How fast is an object moving?
  • What causes an object to accelerate?
  • How much energy does a moving object have?
  • How does sound travel through air?
  • Why do planets move around the Sun?

2. Measurement in Physics

Measurement is essential because it allows scientists to describe observations clearly and compare results. A measurement must include both a numerical value and a unit.

Measurement = Number + Unit

Example: 5.0 meters, 12 seconds, 3.5 kilograms

Without units, numbers have no clear physical meaning. For example, saying “the distance is 10” is incomplete because we do not know whether it means 10 meters, 10 kilometers, or 10 centimeters.

3. SI Base Units

The International System of Units, or SI, provides a standard set of units used in science.

Physical Quantity SI Unit Symbol
Length meter m
Mass kilogram kg
Time second s
Temperature kelvin K
Electric Current ampere A
Amount of Substance mole mol
Luminous Intensity candela cd
Remember: In most mechanics topics, the most common base units are meter, kilogram, and second.

4. Derived Quantities

Derived quantities are formed by combining base quantities.

Quantity Meaning SI Unit
Area length × width m²
Volume length × width × height m³
Speed distance ÷ time m/s
Acceleration change in velocity ÷ time m/s²
Force mass × acceleration N or kg·m/s²
Key Idea: If a unit is made from two or more base units, it is usually a derived unit.

5. Scalar and Vector Quantities

Physical quantities may be classified as scalars or vectors.

Type Description Examples
Scalar Has magnitude only mass, time, speed, temperature, energy
Vector Has magnitude and direction displacement, velocity, acceleration, force

Example:

  • Speed: 20 m/s
  • Velocity: 20 m/s east

Velocity is a vector because it includes direction.

6. Unit Conversion

Unit conversion allows us to express the same measurement using different units. A common method is dimensional analysis, where units are cancelled like algebraic quantities.

Useful Conversions:
  • 1 km = 1000 m
  • 1 m = 100 cm
  • 1 hour = 60 minutes
  • 1 minute = 60 seconds
  • 1 kg = 1000 g

Sample Conversion

Convert 4.5 km to meters.

4.5 km × 1000 m / 1 km = 4500 m

The kilometer unit cancels, leaving the answer in meters.

7. Accuracy and Precision

Accuracy and precision describe the quality of measurements.

Term Meaning
Accuracy How close a measurement is to the accepted or true value
Precision How close repeated measurements are to one another

Suppose the accepted mass of an object is 100.0 g.

  • 99.9 g, 100.0 g, 100.1 g → accurate and precise
  • 95.0 g, 95.1 g, 95.2 g → precise but not accurate
  • 90 g, 103 g, 115 g → not precise and not very accurate

8. Significant Figures

Significant figures show the meaningful digits in a measurement. They help communicate the precision of the measuring instrument.

Measurement Significant Figures Reason
45.2 m 3 All nonzero digits count.
0.0061 kg 2 Leading zeros do not count.
7.00 s 3 Trailing zeros after a decimal count.
1200 m Ambiguous Trailing zeros without a decimal may be unclear.
Important: Significant figures prevent us from reporting answers with more precision than the data actually supports.

Review Checkpoint

1. What two parts must every measurement have?

A measurement must have a numerical value and a unit.

2. Is 15 m/s north a scalar or vector quantity?

It is a vector because it has both magnitude and direction.

3. Convert 250 cm to meters.

100 cm = 1 m
250 cm ÷ 100 = 2.50 m

4. A student records measurements of 2.10 m, 2.11 m, and 2.09 m. Are the measurements precise?

Yes. The measurements are close to one another, so they are precise.

5. How many significant figures are in 0.00840?

There are 3 significant figures: 8, 4, and the final 0 after the decimal.

Practice 4: A car travels 12 km in 10 minutes. Convert the distance to meters and time to seconds, then calculate the average speed in m/s.

Distance: 12 km × 1000 = 12,000 m
Time: 10 min × 60 = 600 s

Average speed = distance ÷ time
Average speed = 12,000 m ÷ 600 s = 20 m/s

Final Chapter Summary

  • Physics studies matter, energy, motion, forces, and interactions.
  • Measurement allows physical observations to be described using numbers and units.
  • The SI system provides standard units used in science.
  • Base quantities include length, mass, time, temperature, electric current, amount of substance, and luminous intensity.
  • Derived quantities are made from combinations of base quantities.
  • Scalars have magnitude only; vectors have magnitude and direction.
  • Dimensional analysis is used to convert units.
  • Accuracy means closeness to the true value.
  • Precision means closeness of repeated measurements to each other.
  • Significant figures communicate the precision of measurements.
