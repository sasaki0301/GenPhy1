Chapter 7 Summary — Fluid Mechanics

Chapter Overview

In this chapter, we explored how fluids exert pressure and how fluids can create forces that allow objects to float, sink, or move. We examined pressure in fluids, Pascal's Principle, Archimedes' Principle, and Bernoulli's Principle. Together, these concepts explain many technologies and natural phenomena, from hydraulic lifts and submarine design to airplane flight and ship buoyancy.

Big Idea: Fluids can exert pressure, transmit forces, create buoyant forces, and produce pressure differences that influence motion.

1. Pressure

Pressure measures how concentrated a force is over a given area.

P = F/A
Where:
  • P = Pressure (Pa)
  • F = Force (N)
  • A = Area (m²)
For the same force, a smaller area produces greater pressure.
Situation Pressure
Large Force, Small Area High Pressure
Small Force, Large Area Low Pressure

2. Applications of Pressure

  • Knife blades
  • Needles
  • Nails
  • Snowshoes
  • Tank tracks
  • Hydraulic machinery
Reducing contact area increases pressure, while increasing area decreases pressure.

3. Pressure in Fluids

Fluids exert pressure in all directions.

Fluid pressure increases with depth because more fluid exists above deeper points.

P = ρgh
Where:
  • ρ = Density of fluid
  • g = Gravitational acceleration
  • h = Depth
The deeper you go in a fluid, the greater the pressure.

4. Effects of Increasing Depth

Depth Pressure
Shallow Lower
Deep Higher

This explains:

  • Why divers feel greater pressure underwater.
  • Why submarines require strong hulls.
  • Why dams are thicker at the bottom.

5. Pascal's Principle

Pascal's Principle states that pressure applied to a confined fluid is transmitted equally throughout the fluid.

Pressure applied anywhere in a confined fluid is transmitted equally in all directions.
P₁ = P₂
F₁/A₁ = F₂/A₂

6. Hydraulic Systems

Hydraulic systems use Pascal's Principle to multiply force.

Examples include:
  • Hydraulic lifts
  • Hydraulic brakes
  • Excavators
  • Bulldozers
  • Dentist chairs
A larger output piston can produce a much larger force than the input piston.

7. Buoyancy

Buoyancy is the upward force exerted by a fluid on an immersed object.

This force exists because pressure at the bottom of an object is greater than pressure at the top.

Pressure differences within a fluid create an upward buoyant force.

8. Archimedes' Principle

Archimedes' Principle states that the buoyant force acting on an object equals the weight of the displaced fluid.

Fb = ρVg
Where:
  • Fb = Buoyant Force
  • ρ = Fluid Density
  • V = Volume Displaced
  • g = Gravitational Acceleration
The greater the volume displaced, the greater the buoyant force.

9. Floating and Sinking

Condition Result
Buoyant Force > Weight Object Rises
Buoyant Force = Weight Object Floats or Suspended
Buoyant Force < Weight Object Sinks
Objects float when their average density is less than the density of the fluid.

10. Density and Buoyancy

Density Comparison Behavior
Object less dense than fluid Floats
Object equal density to fluid Suspended
Object more dense than fluid Sinks

Examples:

  • Wood floats in water.
  • Ice floats in water.
  • Steel nails sink.
  • Ships float because their average density is lower than water.

11. Bernoulli's Principle

Bernoulli discovered that pressure and fluid speed are related.

As fluid speed increases, pressure decreases.

This principle explains many fluid flow phenomena.

12. Applications of Bernoulli's Principle

  • Airplane wings
  • Perfume atomizers
  • Venturi meters
  • Carburetors
  • Fast-moving fluids in pipes
  • Roofs lifted by strong winds

13. Airplane Wing Lift

Air moves faster over the curved top surface of a wing than beneath it.

According to Bernoulli's Principle:

  • Faster air → Lower pressure
  • Slower air → Higher pressure

The pressure difference produces lift.

Pressure differences created by moving fluids can generate upward forces.

Chapter Review Checkpoint

1. What happens to pressure when area decreases but force remains constant?

Pressure increases.

2. Why does fluid pressure increase with depth?

Because more fluid above creates greater weight and pressure.

3. What principle explains hydraulic lifts?

Pascal's Principle.

4. What determines whether an object floats or sinks?

The relationship between buoyant force, weight, and density.

5. According to Bernoulli's Principle, what happens to pressure when fluid speed increases?

Pressure decreases.

Final Chapter Summary

  • Pressure is force per unit area.
  • Pressure increases when force increases or area decreases.
  • Fluid pressure increases with depth.
  • Pascal's Principle explains hydraulic force multiplication.
  • Buoyant force is caused by pressure differences in fluids.
  • Archimedes' Principle relates buoyant force to displaced fluid.
  • Density determines whether objects float, sink, or remain suspended.
  • Bernoulli's Principle states that faster-moving fluids exert lower pressure.
  • Pressure differences in moving fluids explain lift and many engineering applications.
  • Fluid mechanics helps explain ships, submarines, airplanes, hydraulic systems, and countless real-world technologies.
