Temperature, Heat, and Thermal Expansion

Learning Objectives

  • Differentiate temperature from heat.
  • Explain thermal equilibrium.
  • Convert between Celsius, Fahrenheit, and Kelvin scales.
  • Describe the three methods of heat transfer.
  • Explain thermal expansion using particle theory.
  • Calculate thermal expansion in solids.
  • Apply thermal concepts to engineering and everyday situations.

1. Why Do Railroad Tracks Have Gaps?

Have you ever noticed small gaps between sections of railroad tracks or bridges?

Engineers intentionally leave these spaces because metals expand when heated and contract when cooled.

Without these gaps, the rails could bend, buckle, or even break during hot weather.

Heat causes most materials to expand, while cooling causes them to contract.

Expansion gaps prevent railroad tracks from buckling during hot weather.

2. What is Temperature?

Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of the particles in a substance.

The faster the particles move, the higher the temperature.

The slower the particles move, the lower the temperature.

Temperature tells us how energetic the particles of a substance are.

Particle View

  • Hot object → particles move faster.
  • Cold object → particles move slower.

Higher temperature corresponds to faster particle motion.

3. Heat vs Temperature

Students often confuse heat and temperature, but they are not the same thing.

Temperature Heat
Measures average particle energy Energy transferred because of temperature difference
Measured in °C, °F, K Measured in Joules (J)
Property of a substance Energy in transit
Temperature measures how hot something is. Heat is energy flowing from hot objects to cold objects.

4. Thermal Equilibrium

Suppose you place a cold spoon inside a cup of hot coffee.

Heat flows from the coffee into the spoon.

Eventually both reach the same temperature.

At this point, thermal equilibrium has been achieved.

Thermal equilibrium occurs when two objects reach the same temperature and no net heat transfer occurs.

5. Temperature Scales

Scale Freezing Point of Water Boiling Point of Water
Celsius 0°C 100°C
Fahrenheit 32°F 212°F
Kelvin 273.15 K 373.15 K

6. Temperature Conversion

Celsius to Kelvin

K = °C + 273.15

Kelvin to Celsius

°C = K - 273.15

Celsius to Fahrenheit

°F = (9/5)(°C) + 32

Fahrenheit to Celsius

°C = (5/9)(°F - 32)

7. Example: Temperature Conversion

Example 1

Convert 25°C to Kelvin.

K = 25 + 273.15

K = 298.15 K

Answer: 298.15 K

Example 2

Convert 80°C to Fahrenheit.

°F = (9/5)(80) + 32

°F = 176°F

Answer: 176°F

8. Methods of Heat Transfer

Heat always flows from a warmer object to a cooler object.

There are three ways heat can be transferred.

9. Conduction

Conduction is the transfer of heat through direct contact.

Particles collide and transfer energy to neighboring particles.

Examples:
  • Metal spoon in hot soup
  • Touching a hot pan
  • Ironing clothes

Heat travels through direct contact during conduction.

10. Convection

Convection occurs in fluids (liquids and gases).

Warm fluid becomes less dense and rises while cooler fluid sinks.

Examples:
  • Boiling water
  • Ocean currents
  • Atmospheric circulation

Convection currents transfer heat in fluids.

11. Radiation

Radiation transfers heat through electromagnetic waves.

Unlike conduction and convection, radiation does not require a medium.

Examples:
  • Heat from the Sun
  • Campfire warmth
  • Infrared heaters

The Sun transfers energy to Earth through radiation.

12. Thermal Expansion

When materials are heated, their particles move faster and spread farther apart.

As a result, the material expands.

Most solids, liquids, and gases expand when heated and contract when cooled.

13. Linear Expansion Equation

ΔL = αL₀ΔT
Where:
  • ΔL = Change in length
  • α = Coefficient of linear expansion
  • L₀ = Original length
  • ΔT = Temperature change

14. Example: Thermal Expansion

Example 3

A steel rail 25 m long experiences a temperature increase of 40°C.

The coefficient of linear expansion is:

α = 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ /°C

Find the increase in length.

ΔL = αL₀ΔT

ΔL = (1.2 × 10⁻⁵)(25)(40)

ΔL = 0.012 m

ΔL = 1.2 cm

Answer: 1.2 cm increase

15. Engineering Applications and Advanced Examples

Challenge Problem 1

A steel bridge span is 120 m long at 15°C.

If the temperature reaches 45°C, determine the increase in length.

Use:

α = 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ /°C

ΔT = 30°C

ΔL = αL₀ΔT

ΔL = (1.2×10⁻⁵)(120)(30)

ΔL = 0.0432 m

ΔL = 4.32 cm

Challenge Problem 2 (Pisay Style)

An aluminum rod and a steel rod have the same original length.

Both are heated by 80°C.

The coefficient of expansion of aluminum is larger than that of steel.

Which rod expands more and why?

The aluminum rod expands more because its coefficient of expansion is larger. According to: ΔL = αL₀ΔT A larger α produces a larger change in length.

Lesson Summary

  • Temperature measures average particle kinetic energy.
  • Heat is energy transferred because of temperature differences.
  • Thermal equilibrium occurs when temperatures become equal.
  • Heat transfers through conduction, convection, and radiation.
  • Most materials expand when heated and contract when cooled.
  • Thermal expansion is described by ΔL = αL₀ΔT.
  • Engineers account for thermal expansion when designing bridges, railroads, and buildings.
